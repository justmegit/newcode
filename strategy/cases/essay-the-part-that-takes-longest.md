# The part that takes longest
*Essay · draft v1, 24 Oct 2026 · companion to the cases · no client named, no figures*

---

Every case we write has a section called "what was harder than planned". Read them side by side and the pattern is hard to miss. None of them is about the technology working.

A management team received a clear finding about the leads nobody followed up, with the funnel open for anyone to read. Nothing changed. Marketing cannot settle an argument about lead quality from a dashboard, and nobody had agreed to ask the prospects.

An HR team now receives applications through a platform built for them. They still work outside it. The applications reach the right director; the stages are never moved. The tool exists. The routine does not, yet.

Field technicians were asked to send photos of their work through a simple form. Weeks passed and nothing arrived. Nobody had refused. Nobody had made it part of the job either.

A partner's feedback, promised for a launch, did not come in time. The calendar of a project is partly written by people who are not in it.

The sources a market report depends on changed without notice, and the AI service it uses failed on the day it was due. The report went out because a person checked it and had a second route ready.

And one of our own automations stopped running while still reporting that it was active, which is precisely the failure we had been hired to remove.

Two things follow from this.

The first is that the technology is rarely the bottleneck any more. Building is fast, and getting faster. What is slow is the part that depends on people changing what they do on Monday morning: a director who has to own a next step, an HR team that has to move a candidate in a new place, a technician who has to think of the content team while on a client's site. That is where most of the time goes, and where most projects quietly stop.

The second is that this part can be designed, but not delegated to a tool. It needs an owner on the client's side, named before the build starts. It needs the people who will live with the process in the room early, not shown the result at the end. And it needs champions: one or two people in the operation who make the new way normal, so that the rest follow a colleague rather than a memo.

So when we say a piece of work is finished, we mean something specific. Not that the system runs. That the people it was built for are using it without us.

Technology does not transform a company. Adopted technology does.

*From problem to process.*
