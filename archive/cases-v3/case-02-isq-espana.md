# Case 2 — They asked for a hire. The work had no process to hand over.
*Validates: the core thesis, from problem to process · Layer 2 (Transform), with Layer 3 (Advise) running alongside*
*Status: draft v2, 24 Oct 2026 · revised after one red-team pass · named client: publish only with ISQ España's written approval of the text and every figure · every figure traced in `evidence-ledger-isq.csv`*

---

**Context.** ISQ España is the Spanish subsidiary of a Portuguese engineering, inspection and certification group, and an accredited inspection body. Its customers buy because a regulation, a deadline or an incident forces them to, never because of a post. Communications ran on one person: social media, job ads, internal notices. The role was changing hands, and the country manager asked us to find a replacement in Madrid.

**Problem.** The request was a hire. The real problem was that the work only existed while someone did it by hand, and nobody could see when it stopped.

- An automatic birthday greeting to staff had sent nothing since April while reporting success: **38** greetings missed, and no log to show it.
- The monthly market report was compiled by hand and counted competitors' clients and executives as competitor activity, which inflated July's figures by **7%**.
- The LinkedIn page reached people but did not bring them in: **141** page visits from **16,156** impressions in 90 days. Only **25%** of followers were in Spain, and **9%** were decision-makers.
- AI-assisted planning produced claims the company could not stand behind. Checked against the published service catalogue, **5 of 9** proposed topics claimed services ISQ does not sell. None was published. For a company whose product is the fact it signs, one wrong claim costs more than a missed post.

Two constraints shaped the work: AI use was limited to tools approved by the group, and client documents could not leave the company's Microsoft environment.

**Strategy.** Not a full-time hire. A junior operator from our team runs the daily work, and a senior consultant redesigns each task as a process the client owns. Three rules hold it together:

1. Code computes, AI drafts, a person approves. The AI never produces a figure or a regulatory reference; where data is missing, the draft says so and goes to a person.
2. The service catalogue decides what can be said in the first person, before a topic enters the plan.
3. Every process reports its own failures to a person, in plain language, before anyone has to ask.

**Execution.**

- **Internal notices.** Rebuilt with a log and a weekly check that flags a quiet week: **three greetings** sent on schedule in the first three weeks, each logged.
- **Market intelligence.** A monthly report that crosses four sources: competitor company pages, the public procurement register, accreditation changes and its own history. It surfaced public contract awards that competitors had not mentioned in any post. It also refused to report a **+167%** jump in competitor activity that came from how earlier months were collected, not from the market.
- **Content.** Approval moved from a weekly back-and-forth on each piece to theme-setting every two months and a monthly review. The country manager approves each piece where the team already works and can reschedule or edit it until it goes out. From approval to the live post: **under a minute**, with no manual step.
- **Recruitment.** Each job ad published on the website produces three LinkedIn drafts for review. Candidates are summarised from certifications quoted word for word from each CV, with no score and no ranking.

**Process installed.** Weekly collection and checks, a monthly intelligence report validated by the operator, a monthly content review and two-monthly theme setting with the country manager. The operator runs it, the country manager approves, we maintain it. Each role has written operating instructions.

**Result.** The communications function has run since [FILL: month] without a hire, at [FILL: cost as a share of a junior hire in Spain, with the client's consent to state it]. One post featuring an inspector, with the inspector's agreement, reached **94%** of the audience of the best technical post and brought **5x** the page visits, and **16 of 31** of that month's new followers: one post, one month, not a trend. It gave management a number to decide on instead of an opinion.

**What did not work.**

- Field photos: technicians were asked to send pictures of their work through a simple form. After **three weeks** nothing had arrived. The bottleneck was adoption, not technology, and illustrative images are now generated instead.
- Recruitment: the system receives and routes applications, but the habit of moving them through their stages does not exist yet. A tool does not create a routine.

---

**Three questions to ask your team tomorrow**
1. Which recurring communications task would you notice stopping, and how long would it take you?
2. Does your content claim anything your service catalogue cannot back?
3. When your market monitoring shows a change, can you tell a change in the market from a change in how the data was collected?

### LinkedIn version (~150 words)

A client asked us to find a replacement for their communications person.

The vacancy was not the problem. An automatic birthday greeting had sent nothing for four months while reporting success. The market report counted competitors' clients as competitors. Five of nine proposed content topics claimed services the company does not sell.

So we did not fill the role. We redesigned the work: code computes, AI drafts, a person approves, and every process reports its own failures. A junior operator runs it. The country manager approves each piece, and can stop it, up to the minute it goes out.

One post featuring an inspector brought five times the page visits of a technical post with the same reach. One post, but it gave management a number instead of an opinion.

Not everything worked. Technicians never sent field photos. Adoption is the hard part.

Before you hire for a task, ask whether it is a process.
